Porcelain Tile for Saudi Hotel Lobbies: High-Traffic Procurement Checklist

A Saudi hotel-lobby porcelain procurement guide covering traffic, finish, slip questions, mock-ups, batch control, cleaning, replacements and RFQ evidence.

2026-11-09 · LuxeCeram Export Team

Market context: why lobby specifications deserve their own decision

Saudi Arabia continues to expand its hospitality inventory, with Knight Frank reporting a large pipeline of hotel rooms under construction or advanced planning in 2026. For a tile buyer, the useful conclusion is not simply that more hotels are being built. It is that lobby finishes will be compared under tighter project schedules, heavier operational expectations and more formal approval processes. A lobby is a public-facing zone, but it is also an operating surface that must survive luggage wheels, housekeeping equipment, tracked-in dust, cleaning chemicals and repeated maintenance. Procurement should therefore separate visual preference from technical acceptance.

Start with the actual traffic and operating pattern

Write down how the lobby will be used before asking suppliers for options. A business hotel with constant trolley movement, a resort with sand and pool traffic, and a luxury city property with large-format stone-look surfaces create different risks. Estimate peak movement, entrance contamination, cleaning frequency, expected luggage-wheel traffic and whether the same finish continues into corridors or retail areas. This operating note becomes part of the RFQ so every supplier is answering the same use case instead of showing only attractive samples.

Define surface and slip questions without relying on one label

A polished surface can support a premium visual concept, but a lobby may include entrance zones, transitions from exterior paving, café edges or areas exposed to wet shoes. Do not accept a generic statement such as “anti-slip” as the complete answer. Ask for the exact test method and declared result for the offered surface, then have the project consultant confirm whether that evidence is appropriate for the intended zone. Where the design uses more than one finish, record which finish belongs to which location. The goal is not to choose the roughest tile; it is to make the surface decision traceable.

Control format, flatness and joint planning for large public areas

Large lobby floors make alignment problems visible. Before approving a large format, confirm actual dimensions, rectification, thickness, allowable dimensional variation and the planned joint width. Ask the installer or consultant to confirm substrate tolerance, movement-joint strategy and whether the selected format creates practical cutting or handling issues around columns, reception desks and curved boundaries. If multiple sizes are combined, require a layout drawing rather than assuming nominal dimensions will coordinate perfectly.

Use a physical mock-up as a procurement checkpoint

A small sample confirms colour and surface, but it does not show repeated pattern, grout tone, reflection, lighting or installation quality across a large lobby. Build a mock-up using the actual tile, intended grout colour, proposed joint width and representative lighting where practical. Record the approved product code, shade or batch reference, finish and sample date. Photograph the approved mock-up and link it to the purchase order. This gives procurement, site and supplier teams one shared reference if later deliveries are questioned.

Compare suppliers on the same evidence set

Ask every shortlisted supplier for the same package: technical datasheet, product code, nominal and actual size information, finish, packing details, pallet data, production lead time, Incoterm, quotation validity, sample reference and any project-specific certificates requested by the consultant. Keep commercial exclusions visible. A cheaper line can become more expensive if packing is weak, replacement quantities are unavailable, or the quotation excludes services assumed by another bidder. One comparison matrix prevents these differences from disappearing inside email threads.

Protect shade, caliber and batch continuity

Hotel lobbies are visually unforgiving when replacement areas do not match. Ask how shade and caliber are controlled and how cartons are marked. If the project needs phased deliveries, confirm whether the supplier can reserve production or maintain traceability across lots. Do not assume that the same product name guarantees an identical later batch. The purchase order should state what identifiers must appear on cartons and shipping documents, and receiving staff should record them before material is distributed around the site.

Plan cleaning and maintenance before final approval

The correct finish is partly a maintenance decision. Ask the operator or housekeeping team how the lobby will be cleaned, what machines or chemicals are expected, and whether textured surfaces create extra cleaning time. Test representative contamination where possible, especially at entrances and food-and-beverage interfaces. Avoid promising stain resistance or chemical performance beyond the manufacturer’s declared evidence. The procurement file should show which cleaning assumptions were used when the surface was approved.

Order replacement stock deliberately

Replacement stock should be a planned quantity, not an afterthought. Consider the total installed area, pattern complexity, likelihood of future localized repairs and storage conditions. The right reserve percentage depends on the project; do not use one universal figure. More important is traceability: reserve cartons should be clearly identified and stored so they are not consumed for unrelated maintenance work. Record their batch information together with the final as-built documents.

RFQ checklist for a Saudi hotel lobby

Before final supplier selection, confirm: exact product and finish; intended lobby zones; technical datasheet; declared slip-test evidence where relevant; format, thickness and rectification; sample and mock-up approval reference; shade/caliber identifiers; packing and pallet details; lead time; Incoterm and payment terms; phased-delivery requirements; replacement-stock plan; cleaning assumptions; and the process for documenting deviations. If any item is unresolved, label it as open rather than allowing an assumption to become a hidden commitment.

Conclusion

For a Saudi hotel lobby, successful tile procurement is not only a design exercise. It is a coordination exercise across architect, consultant, contractor, operator, buyer and supplier. A clear operating brief, comparable evidence, a recorded mock-up and batch traceability reduce the chance that an attractive sample becomes a difficult installation or maintenance problem. Use current project specifications and consultant requirements as the final authority, and keep every approval tied to the exact product version being ordered.
